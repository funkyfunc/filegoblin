Four aspects of expertise which we find particularly relevant to knowledge elicitation are the organization of knowledge, its influence on the representation of problems, characteristic problem-solving strategies, and the tacit nature of the knowledge. In addition to being sensitive to these four aspects of expertise, our methodology attempts to avoid “reductive bias,” one of the pitfalls of advanced learning in complex domains.

# 1. Knowledge Organization

Experts have a great deal of domain-specific knowledge, compared to novices. However, more than quantity of knowledge, it is the qualitative differences that have implications for knowledge elicitation.⁴ At a macro level, a variety of studies (e.g., Chi *et al.*⁵) have found expert knowledge to have a generally hierarchical organization (i.e., categories with subcategories), although many other types of relationships are also present, depending on the particular domain. All of this domain-specific knowledge is tightly interconnected with many associations among items. By contrast, novices do not yet have their domain-specific knowledge nearly as well organized or tightly integrated.

At the micro level, expert knowledge is stored as organized “chunks” of frequently occurring patterns or schemas.⁴ In the case of chess, for example, Simon and Gilmartin⁶ have estimated that grand masters have acquired on the order of 50,000 different patterns or chunks of chess information, each of which is associated with one or more strategies used in play. An important implication of this chunking or schema formation is that it enables an expert to quickly recognize patterns in the information encountered during problem solving. Part of schema formation is the association of functional characteristics and action sequences relevant to that particular pattern.

While expert knowledge has abstract dimensions, LaFrance⁴ points out that it is important to recognize that experience is grounded in specific situations or “cases” that have been encountered in the past. While abstractions obviously arise from those specific cases, we must not lose sight of the fact that they remain an important part of the memory organization of an expert and provide an important basis for problem solving.⁷
